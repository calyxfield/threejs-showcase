How the force is calculated

Fixed delay uses the time selected on the slider. Finite speed solves the travel-time equation separately for each body: cτ = |rⱼ(t − τ) − rᵢ(t)|. The source position and its travel time agree with each other. As the bodies separate, the travel delay can grow beyond six seconds. Signal speed is in distance units per simulated second, on a logarithmic slider. The initial orbital speed of each body is about 0.5 units/s.

Each body is pulled from its current position toward the other body’s position τ seconds ago. Both the direction and distance used in the force come from that pair of positions. Hollow markers show those past positions. White force arrows show direction only. Colored velocity arrows show direction and magnitude, with a common visual scale. The numerical readouts give each body’s x and y velocity components.

aᵢ(t) = G mⱼ [rⱼ(t − τ) − rᵢ(t)] / (|rⱼ(t − τ) − rᵢ(t)|² + ε²)³ᐟ²

Equal masses m = 1, G = 1, initial separation = 2, and softening ε = 0.03 in simulation units. The bodies begin with circular-orbit velocities. For times before the simulation starts, their history follows the same circular orbit. A faint dashed circle marks that starting orbit. Trails show the last 30 simulated seconds.

The energy readout shows the change in ordinary instantaneous kinetic plus softened gravitational potential energy, divided by the magnitude of its starting value. It is a diagnostic of the toy force rule, not a relativistic energy accounting.

Finite-speed runs retain their trajectory for up to 300 simulated seconds (or one million history samples), then pause for a reset. They also pause if a source reaches the selected signal speed, or if lowering the speed makes the recorded source motion too fast, because this solver requires a unique retarded source position. Increase c or reset if that happens.

This is a deliberately delayed Newtonian model, not a simulation of general relativity. Its mechanical energy need not be conserved when delay is enabled. In relativity, additional velocity-dependent effects matter. About gravitational aberration.
